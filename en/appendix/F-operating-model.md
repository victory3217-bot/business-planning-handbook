# Appendix F. The Operating Model: Who Runs It, in What Order, by What Rules

> This appendix is supplementary material drawn from the standard lecture materials that organize the author's teaching and consulting results, not from the handbook body (which is Knowledge-Module based). It is a draft built on thin source material. Its scope is limited to the tools and questions for checking the operating structure; it contains no operating cases. It also could not include content that directly addresses production, quality, logistics, staffing, or organization.

## Questions This Chapter Answers

- Where does the operating model sit after the business model and the execution plan?
- What tools are there for checking who does what, in what order, who is accountable, and what the exceptions are?
- What must you settle before drawing lanes and roles?
- When demand grows, where do supply, throughput, repeatability, and the cash timing gap get blocked?
- What are the minimum questions for quickly checking an operating structure?

## 1. Core Concepts

This appendix does not explain every way of running a business. It covers what the standard lecture materials group under the operating model: the place the operating model occupies after the business model and the execution plan, the tools for checking who does what, in what order, and by what rules, and the questions that ask about the timing gap among supply, throughput, and cash. Where CH05 (the business model) sets the structure of key resources, key activities, and partnerships, this appendix is a frame for checking how work actually passes from one party to the next on top of that structure.

The boundaries are as follows. Key resources, key activities, key partnerships, and the internal/external split of activities belong to CH05. The calculation of cost, price, break-even, and cash flow belongs to CH06; sellable quantity and projected financials to CH07; and the translation into an execution plan that writes activities, timing, resources, and costs, along with validation experiments, MVPs, and pivoting, to CH08. Channel selection is in Appendices C and D. Overlapping content is not repeated; it is only pointed to under "Questions handed on to other chapters."

### The operating model is the "way of working" that follows the business model

The lecture materials say that where the business model explains the components a business needs and their relationships, the operating model explains how they actually run. The conceptual flow has four tiers.

| Tier | What it answers |
|---|---|
| Business model | What, and why |
| Execution plan | Activities, time, resources, cost |
| Operating model | Who, in what order, by what rules |
| Operating manual | e.g., contract forms, a distribution-policy manual |

The same place shows up on the business-plan side. The lecture materials organize the questions a business plan answers into four cards — why and how much will you earn, the product and business model, the execution plan and projected financials, and the operating model — and put the operating model last.

The lecture materials caution against merely declaring a key activity such as "product development," and say to make it concrete at the level of activities: who does what, and when. So the first question to put to the operating model is one: can you spell out one key activity of your business as who does what, and when? The execution-plan translation that writes activities, timing, resources, and costs on a single sheet is covered in CH08, and this appendix does not repeat it.

## 2. Key Frameworks and Mental Models

### Tools for seeing operations: who, order, rules, exceptions

The lecture materials list six tools for drawing the operating model and explain that they show the same operation from different angles. This appendix includes only the five whose definitions were confirmed. The sixth, Workflow, has only a picture space and a note box in the original, so its definition could not be confirmed.

| Tool | What it shows | Use the lecture materials give |
|---|---|---|
| Swimlane | The person in charge at each step (the lane) and the work flow | When organizing collaboration across roles and departments |
| SOP | Standard operating procedure | When you want repeated work done the same way |
| RACI | For each task, the roles that perform (R), are accountable (A), are consulted (C), and are informed (I) | When settling who is accountable |
| Process Variants | How the same work actually proceeds along several branches | When confirming that a process is more complex than it seems |
| Decision Flow | The decision structure | When attaching it per business unit after organizing the Process Variants |

These definitions rest on the lecture materials' plain-language shortening of outside explanations. The "Use the lecture materials give" column is the column the lecture materials themselves describe as a lecture-use summary.

#### Swimlane: see who is in charge of which step

A Swimlane is a diagram that visualizes and conveys a process and its components. The lecture materials explain that it helps team members understand their roles and responsibilities more clearly and makes collaboration across departments easier. To read it: each horizontal or vertical strip (lane) is a person in charge, each box is a task, and a diamond is a decision branch. With this diagram you can see how work passes from one person in charge to the next, and use it to find handoff points and gaps in accountability. The check question is whether each lane has at least one person in charge.

#### SOP: make the same work get done the same way

An SOP is a document that writes the standard operating procedure step by step, and it is the basis for operating manuals such as contract forms and a distribution-policy manual. The SOP form the lecture materials show puts step-by-step work, key points, easy mistakes, and photos and diagrams on one page. The check question is whether steps and standards are written so that even someone doing it for the first time reaches the same result.

#### RACI: settle accountability for each task

A RACI is a table that settles, for every task and decision, who performs it, who is accountable, and who is consulted and informed. R is the role that actually performs the work, A is the role that is accountable, C is the role whose opinion is asked, and I is the role that is told the result. When the accountable person and the performer are the same, write A/R. The lecture materials' check question is which tasks in your business have accountability missing or overlapping between two people, and the practice standard is to flag a task with no A or with two. For a one-person business, they advise using outside partners as the columns instead of roles.

#### Process Variants and Decision Flow: organize the exceptions and the flow stands up

Process Variants show that the same work proceeds along several branches, so a process is more complex than it seems. The original explanation is that Variants show how much we underestimate a process's complexity. The lecture materials explain that organizing the Variants and the decision structure and attaching them per business unit is the Decision Flow. The question is: what is the exception that arises most often in my core work, and who decides it?

#### The order in which to use the tools

The lecture materials' exercise uses these tools in the following order. Write one core task in four steps, and for each step write the person in charge (R), the final accountable person (A), and, if there is an exception, one line of decision criteria. Then take those four steps as tasks and write R, A, C, and I against four roles. The lecture materials advise organizing it as a table first, before drawing a diagram.

### Draw lanes and roles on top of the internal/external split

The lecture materials divide key activities into those you perform internally and those you entrust to outsiders, and say that for the parts that depend on outsiders you should also look at the effect on cost, bargaining power, and supply stability. They then connect this by saying the lanes of a Swimlane and the roles of a RACI are drawn on top of this split. The internal/external split and the check of dependence on partners are covered in CH05, and this appendix does not repeat them. What this appendix adds is the order. First decide which activities you do internally and which you entrust outside, and on top of that draw who is in charge of which step.

### Ask about the timing gap among supply, throughput, and cash

The lecture materials for the F&B industry ask about a business's readiness with four questions: can you sell (SELL), can you make (MAKE), can you keep earnings (EARN), and can you repeat (REPEAT). The closer all four come to "yes" at once, the lower the risk, and the lecture materials say the purpose of the checklist is not perfect preparation but finding the riskiest hypothesis. For each question the judgment is marked ○, △, or ×, with one line of reasons, and the marks are not added up into a score. The lecture materials state that the four questions are an application the original condensed from business-model fit and validation design. This appendix covers only the two of the four that belong to operations, MAKE and REPEAT. SELL asks about customer, value, and channel, so it belongs to CH03, CH04, and Appendices C and D, and EARN to CH06. The lecture materials use this frame within the F&B industry and do not say whether it can be used as is in other industries.

**Can you make it?** However good the product, the business does not hold if you cannot supply the quantity wanted at the time wanted. The lecture materials check four things: quality, quantity, supply, and partners (Quality and Delivery). For online and B2B, they stress that packaging and delivery are part of product quality.

**Throughput.** Taking the take-out type as an example, the lecture materials say that daily orders cannot exceed peak-time throughput, so look at throughput before revenue. The question is not "how many do you want to sell per day" but first "how many can you actually handle at peak time," and demand and throughput are designed together. The same lecture materials say that what matters about the required sales volume from break-even is less the number itself than whether it is possible with your channels and production capacity, and advise returning to the readiness check if the required sales volume exceeds channel throughput. The break-even calculation belongs to CH06.

**Can you repeat it?** Look at repeatability before scalability. The question is where it will get blocked first when sales grow, and learners are asked where it would get blocked if orders doubled starting tomorrow.

**The cash timing gap.** Because of inventory, prepayment, and production cycles, money goes out first and comes in later. The flow the lecture materials draw has five tiers: paying for raw materials, production and packaging, holding inventory, sales, and receipt of payment. They do not go deep into an accounting definition of the cash needed to hold out until revenue stabilizes, and instead have learners understand it as a "cash gap." The lens for looking at profit and cash flow together is covered in CH06.

## 3. Application Process

1. **Spell out one key activity.** Write it as who does what, and when.
2. **Split internal from external.** Decide which activities you do internally and which you entrust outside.
3. **Write one core task in four steps.** For each step write the person in charge (R), the final accountable person (A), and, if there is an exception, one line of decision criteria.
4. **Organize it as a RACI table.** Take the four steps as tasks and write R, A, C, and I against four roles. Flag any task with no A or with two. Organize it as a table first, then draw the diagram.
5. **Check exceptions and procedures.** Write the exception that arises most often and who decides it, and for repeated work write the steps and standards so that even a first-timer reaches the same result.
6. **Ask where it will get blocked.** Check whether supply and throughput can support demand, where it gets blocked if orders double, and whether you have looked at the cash timing gap.

## 4. Common Judgment Errors

| Error | What the lecture materials say |
|---|---|
| Only declaring the key activity | Make it concrete at the level of activities: who does what, and when |
| Leaving tasks whose accountability is empty or overlapping | Flag a task with no A or with two, and look for tasks where accountability is empty or overlaps |
| Drawing the flow with no exceptions | A process is more complex than it seems. Ask about the exceptions that arise most often and who decides them |
| Keeping the procedure in people's heads | Check that steps and standards are written so that even a first-timer reaches the same result |
| Looking only at demand and not at throughput | Daily orders cannot exceed peak-time throughput. Design demand and throughput together |
| Assuming a good product is enough and not looking at supply | If you cannot supply the quantity wanted at the time wanted, the business does not hold |
| Looking at scalability before repeatability | Look at repeatability first, and ask where it gets blocked first when sales grow |
| Looking only at profit and not at the cash timing gap | Money goes out first and comes in later, so a cash gap can arise |

This table gathers the content above; it is not a table the lecture materials itemized as "errors."

## 5. Practical Application Questions

- Can you spell out one key activity of your business as who does what, and when?
- Does each lane have a person in charge and an accountable person? Which tasks have accountability empty or overlapping between two people?
- Are steps and standards written so that even a first-timer reaches the same result?
- What is the exception that arises most often in your core work, and who decides it?
- How many orders can you actually handle at peak time? Where would it get blocked if orders doubled starting tomorrow?
- Do you have the cash to hold out through the gap where money goes out first and comes in later?

The questions handed on to other chapters are as follows.

- CH05: On top of the structure that splits key activities into those done internally and those entrusted outside, can the lanes and roles be drawn without contradiction? If a partner drops out, which activity stops?
- CH06: Have the costs and the cash timing gap that this operating flow creates been reflected in the price structure and break-even?
- CH07: Is the projected sales quantity possible within our throughput and supply range?
- CH08: Where does an execution plan that writes activities, timing, resources, and costs conflict with these operating rules, and which hypothesis will you confirm with the smallest possible action?
- Appendices C and D: If you leave channel operating rules as an operating manual, like a distribution-policy manual, do they contradict the allocation of channel roles?

The minimum questions for quickly checking an operating structure are four.

- Who: does each lane have a person in charge and an accountable person?
- In what order: is the core work written out as steps?
- By what rules: are there exceptions, someone who decides them, and a procedure even a first-timer can follow?
- Where does it get blocked: have you looked at throughput and the cash timing gap?

## Key Takeaways

- The operating model is the explanation of "who, in what order, by what rules" that sits after the business model and the execution plan.
- Do not leave a key activity as a declaration; spell it out at the level of activities.
- Check who is in charge and the order with a Swimlane, accountability with a RACI, the procedure for repeated work with an SOP, and exceptions with Process Variants and Decision Flow.
- Draw lanes and roles on top of the internal/external split.
- Whether operations actually run is asked through whether you can supply, whether throughput supports demand, whether you can repeat, and whether you can withstand the cash timing gap. These questions come from the F&B-industry lecture materials, so they need confirmation before use in other industries.
- This appendix is a draft limited to tools and questions for checking the operating structure. Operating cases and content on production, quality, logistics, staffing, and organization are empty.
