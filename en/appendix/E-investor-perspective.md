# Appendix E. The Investor Perspective: What Investors Read and Ask in a Plan

> This appendix is supplementary material drawn from the standard lecture materials that organize the author's teaching and consulting results, not from the handbook body (which is Knowledge-Module based). It is a draft that rests on a single set of standard lecture materials, and the check that would tie its basis to the original sources page by page is not yet complete. This appendix is not legal, tax, or investment advice, and it does not recommend any particular investment decision.

## Questions This Chapter Answers

- What changes when you add the investor's perspective to a business plan?
- Is taking investment a choice that fits the goals and criteria you have set?
- What items make up IR materials, and why must they be understandable even when read without a presentation?
- What do investors read technology, product, market, and founding team as evidence of?
- What must a highlight, a risk, an exit scenario, and the investment terms each be written together with?

## 1. Core Concepts

This appendix does not tell you how to raise investment, and it does not calculate enterprise value for you. It covers the perspective investors use when they read a plan, how materials written for fundraising differ from other plans, the structure of IR materials, the frameworks behind investors' questions, and how to write exit scenarios and investment terms together with their basis.

The boundaries are as follows. How to read the projected P&L, the calculation of required funding starting from the low point of cumulative operating losses (the J-curve), and the effective market belong to CH07. This appendix does not repeat those calculations; it only connects them to the questions investors ask. Price, cost, and profit structure are in CH06, the business model in CH05, the definition of customer and buyer in CH03, setting success criteria and the level of growth in CH01, channel judgment in Appendices C and D, and execution and validation in CH08.

### A plan asks about feasibility; IR asks more about growth and exit

The standard lecture materials frame the investor perspective as adding questions about growth and exit to the feasibility a business plan explains. Where a business plan explains "can this business be done?", in front of an investor two more questions are added: how large can the business grow, and how can the invested money be returned?

### Why you take investment, and the price paid in equity

The lecture materials treat fundraising as something that starts from a goal (GOAL) and a reason (WHY). Their reading is that only once you have decided why you want investment can you decide how much to take and from whom. So it is posed as a question: what share of the goal you are trying to achieve will the investment money go toward?

The lecture materials also point out that equity and value are different. Compare 100% of a small company with a part of a large company, and the latter can be worth more. As a company grows, your ownership percentage may shrink while the value of your stake grows. But this is not a conclusion that one side is right. Growth scale is not a default you automatically follow; it is a strategic condition you choose while accepting its price. That taking investment reduces your ownership is something to settle at the start. Choices other than fundraising — staying small, or declining an offer — are judged by the same criterion. That criterion is set in CH01.

### The same business has a different type depending on who reads it

The lecture materials divide plans into three types: the startup commercialization type, the R&D type, and the investment-attraction type. What decides the type is not the form but the evaluator's decision question. Adjusting by type is described as rearranging the outputs of a common core, not writing a new document. What the lecture materials add for the investment-attraction type is as follows.

- Six areas of a self-checklist (Section 2)
- The backbone of IR built with NABC (the pitch structure in Section 2)
- Checkpoints that ask for the basis of sales and supply (Section 2)
- The point that growth is a choice
- The goal and required amount for the next round, and the exit scenario

The question is this: who reads the plan you are writing now, and what decision will that person make?

### Funds come in as debt or equity

In a structural diagram of investing, operating, and financing activities, the lecture materials show how to read the flow: funds come in as borrowed debt or as equity from investment, and are used as assets. Investment is one way of bringing funds in, and borrowing sits alongside it in the diagram. The lecture materials give no accounting definitions, and neither does this appendix.

### An investment firm's funds come in two kinds

The lecture materials split the way investment firms raise money into two. A project fund is formed by recruiting investors (LPs) for a specific investment. A blind fund is set up first, and the investees are found afterward. The lecture materials write that the investment schedule and preferred fields can differ depending on the fund's nature. Conditions of particular funds are not included in this appendix.

## 2. Key Frameworks and Mental Models

### IR materials are both a presentation and a document for review

According to the original text the lecture materials quote, IR materials are not only presentation materials but also documents that an investment firm keeps referring to inside the firm during the investment decision process. So the writer's task is framed this way: the structure must be understandable without the presentation, and its basis must be verifiable. The lecture materials' reading is that anything added orally during the presentation but absent from the materials disappears at the internal review stage.

The lecture materials show the investment process as a staged diagram, but the diagram's source, reference date, and differences between investment firms are unconfirmed, so it is not carried into this appendix. Here we state only that IR materials do not end with the presentation and are read again in later review.

The structure of IR materials proposed by the lecture materials has eight items, from the summary to the attachments. Each item must come with supporting material.

| Item | What it contains (the parentheses of the original the lecture materials quote) |
|---|---|
| Summary | Summary |
| Company status | General, business status, technology-development projects in progress |
| Financial analysis | Summary financials, borrowings, collateral and guarantees |
| Market analysis | Market size and growth, competitive intensity, main market |
| Revenue potential | Characteristics of the main field, revenue simulation |
| Risk analysis | Risks and how to overcome them |
| Investment overview (investment proposal) | Required funds, investment terms |
| Attachments | Reference checks |

The lecture materials state that the original does not say how these eight items correspond to the "eight essentials" in the pitch structure below. They are two different lists.

### An investor's self-checklist has six areas

The lecture materials summarize the investment review perspective in six areas: technology, product, market, founding team, EXIT, and other. Quoting the original, they say that technology, product, market, and team capability are the grounds for gauging the possibility of an EXIT. In other words, investors read the earlier areas as evidence of the possibility of recovering their money. EXIT means the return of the invested money.

| Area | What is checked (the original the lecture materials quote) |
|---|---|
| Technology | Level of technology development, entry barriers, roadmap |
| Product | Customer value, comparative advantage |
| Market | Market size, growth, competitive intensity |
| Founding team | Company vision, founder's reputation, composition of key personnel |
| EXIT | Possibility, EXIT method |
| Other | Required funds, future fundraising plans |

The lecture materials have no evaluation criteria per area (no pass mark or score). This table is a frame for bringing questions to mind; the lecture materials give no basis for saying it is a scoring sheet investors actually use.

### Investment Highlight and FOMO

The lecture materials make the investment highlight a single page that answers "why should we invest?" They quote the original phrase "No FOMO = No Investment." FOMO stands for Fear of Missing Out, the feeling of regret at missing something. A highlight is not a place to say we are good; it is a place to write why this investor would regret missing us. The lecture materials treat FOMO as scarcity with a basis, not exaggeration, and caution against manufacturing FOMO from unsupported figures. The question is this: in our business, what would this investor regret missing, and what supports it?

### Differentiation and entry barriers

Carrying over the original's wording, the lecture materials write that differentiation is created at the points that theory and manuals cannot solve. They list four points of appeal.

- Entry barrier from technological superiority: technology and intellectual property that others find hard to copy
- Entry barrier from the user base: customers and usage data accumulated first
- Product planning
- Attractiveness of the business model

The lecture materials read the first two as barriers and the latter two as supporting them. The question is one of the original's pitch-preparation questions: can you build an entry barrier?

### Risk Point

The lecture materials pair a risk point with the highlight. The original's title is "What is it, and how will it be overcome?", and the lecture materials explain that a risk is not something to hide but something to write together with how it will be overcome. The way of overcoming it must come with a basis and a schedule, and you should not be baselessly optimistic in order to make a risk look smaller.

### Four checkpoints that ask for the basis of sales and supply

The lecture materials gather the CHECK POINT marks from the original slides and arrange them into four perspectives for judging investment potential.

| Checkpoint | Question the lecture materials quote |
|---|---|
| MOQ | Is the supply chain certain enough that you can make what is ordered, or is the sales outlet certain enough that you can hand over the planned volume? |
| Sales | Even if the supply chain and sales outlet are uncertain, can the sales trend meet a fundraising metric? (The BM is still examined.) |
| Strategy | Profit = (price − total cost) × sales quantity |
| Role / use of funds | Where among investing, operating, and financing activities is the investment money used? |

The lecture materials state that the original gives no basis for saying these four are criteria investors actually use. Use them to see first where your business is weakest among the four. Price, cost, and profit structure are covered in CH06, and the basis for sales quantity in CH07 and Appendices C and D.

### Questions about market, sales, and exit

The lecture materials carry over questions from the original IR pitch materials to organize what must be persuaded in front of an investor.

- **Market**: Does the market exist, how large is it, and can you earn a profit within it? How to persuade logically about the effective market is covered in CH07.
- **Sales plan and resources**: Are the market-entry plan for generating revenue and the available resources presented reasonably? Has marketing efficiency been checked per channel? Costs arise from technology-development, product-development, and mass-production plans; are the available resources sufficient? Channel judgment continues in Appendices C and D, and costs and required funds in CH07.
- **After selection and investment**: What quantitative effects are expected after being selected? After the investment is made, how much does the investor take back? What are the goals and strategy up to the next round's growth, and the required amount going forward? Carrying over the original sentence, the lecture materials write that, in the end, the basic components of the Value Proposition must be in order. The caution that the required amount must come from a plan with a basis is the lecture materials' own.
- **Conflict points in the business model**: Where do the interests of this business's users collide? They say to explain this together with who bears the burden on the conflicting side (the payer). This question continues from CH05.

### The three parts of an EXIT appeal connect through numbers

Following the original, the lecture materials divide the EXIT appeal into three parts. The first is a revenue and profit estimate simulation. Revenue is price × quantity and profit is revenue minus costs, estimated on the same basis. This calculation and the required funds belong to CH07; this appendix takes only the lecture materials' point that without an estimation basis it is hard for an investor to picture a return. The second is the roadmap and the third is the investment proposal. The lecture materials say the three parts must connect to one another through numbers.

### Attach an exit scenario to each milestone of the roadmap

Organize an expected scenario of how an EXIT can happen as the company reaches each milestone. The original example the lecture materials cite is a sentence saying the enterprise value rises at a distant point and the investor recovers the money through a sale of existing shares, with the enterprise-value multiple left blank. The lecture materials caution that such a multiple or timing should be written only as an assumption with a basis. The structure of putting timing, method, and scale (how many times the invested money grows) into one sentence is the lecture materials' proposal. They give a sale of existing shares, an M&A, and an IPO as examples of exit methods, but requirements and proportions by method are not included in this appendix.

### An investment proposal pairs the terms with the basis for the valuation

Quoting the original, the lecture materials say to set out the investment terms the company proposes and to state clearly the basis for the valuation.

| Investment term | What to write |
|---|---|
| Amount raised | How much you receive (the basis is the funding plan and the use of funds) |
| Form of investment | In what form you receive it |
| Enterprise value | At what value you are assessed (together with the basis for the calculation) |

The lecture materials give three bases for estimating enterprise value.

- Net income multiplied by a PER
- A relative comparison with the enterprise value at the most recent capital increase or M&A in the same industry (or, if none, in a different industry with a similar PER)
- A check of the equity-given-up ratio, to see whether the share given up for the amount raised is appropriate

The lecture materials state that the original has no procedure for each method, no PER value, and no source or reference date for comparison cases, and this appendix does not write them either.

Write the value before investment and the value after investment separately. The formulas in the original the lecture materials quote are as follows. Pre-money is the number of existing issued shares × price per share, and Post-money is Pre-money + the amount raised. The lecture materials left the actual contract structure, dilutive securities, and tax and legal judgments as matters for experts to confirm.

### How to structure a pitch in front of investors

According to the original sentence the lecture materials quote, this presentation is not to sell your product to customers but to get investors to invest in your business. So the lecture materials put putting yourself in the audience's shoes first. Ask from the audience's standpoint how much weight this investment has in your business's success. The lecture materials do not distinguish between types such as the investment-attraction type and the support-program type.

The lecture materials carry over the original's six principles of business-plan presentation as follows.

| Principle | Key point |
|---|---|
| 1 | The 30-second opening: the audience's standpoint (putting yourself in their shoes) and a hook (track record, achievements) |
| 2 | Value Proposition: Customer · Problem · Solution |
| 3 | Practical benefit of the investment/support: the funds and the profit, the time of recovery, funding by stage, expansion plan |
| 4 | The eight essentials that must not be left out even if you write less |
| 5 | No FOMO = No Investment |
| 6 | Simple slides: no video you cannot handle, keep the tone and manner, no text over images |

The eight essentials are the motive for starting up, market needs, the product, KPIs, market analysis, the business model/revenue model, the team, and the fundraising plan. The lecture materials caution that you write track records and achievements only where there is a basis.

NABC is the backbone of a single pitch page. N is Need (or Problem), A is Approach (or Solution), B is Benefits (Value Proposition), and C is Competitor, and each of the four boxes should be one sentence. Investment reviewers read many documents, so what the item is must be recognized quickly. Carrying over this point of the original, the lecture materials say not to start with a feature list or technical terms. This appendix covers only the structural framework of the pitch, not presentation practice.

## 3. Application Process

1. **Write down the reader and that person's decision.** Write who reads your plan and what decision that person makes.
2. **Settle why you take investment and what it costs.** First settle the goal, the reason, and the price in equity you will accept. Judge the choice of not taking investment by the same criterion.
3. **Check yourself against the six areas.** Read technology, product, market, founding team, EXIT, and other as evidence of the possibility of an EXIT, and find the weakest.
4. **Write the highlight and the risk as a pair.** Write the reason the investor would regret missing you and its basis, and write each risk with how to overcome it, with a basis and a schedule.
5. **Find the weakest of the four checkpoints.** Check which is weakest among MOQ, Sales, Strategy, and Role / use of funds.
6. **Connect the EXIT appeal through numbers.** Check that the revenue and profit estimate, the exit scenario for each milestone, and the investment proposal connect on the same basis. Write multiples and timing only as assumptions with a basis.
7. **Write the investment terms and the basis for the valuation as a pair.** State the amount raised, the form of investment, and the enterprise value with their basis.
8. **Check that it is understandable without a presentation.** See whether supporting material accompanies each of the eight IR items, and whether anything is supplemented only by word of mouth.

## 4. Common Judgment Errors

| Error | What the lecture materials say |
|---|---|
| Supplementing only orally during the presentation | Anything added orally but absent from the materials disappears at the internal review stage |
| Not writing risks, or writing them without a way to overcome them | Write a risk together with how it will be overcome, and do not be baselessly optimistic |
| Manufacturing FOMO from unsupported figures | FOMO is scarcity with a basis |
| Proposing investment terms without a basis | The investment terms and the basis for the valuation are a pair |
| Writing the multiple in an exit scenario without a basis | Write the multiple and timing only when they are assumptions with a basis |
| Using old investment-trend figures as if they were current | The original's investment-trend figures are based on a 2021 report and are not current values |
| Treating growth and fundraising as the default | Growth scale is a strategic condition you choose while accepting its price |

## 5. Practical Application Questions

- Who reads the plan you are writing now, and what decision will that person make?
- What share of the goal you are trying to achieve will the investment money go toward?
- In our business, what would this investor regret missing, and what supports it?
- Can you build an entry barrier?
- Among the questions an investor would ask, where is the basis empty, and what validation will fill it?

The questions handed on to other chapters are as follows.

- CH01: Do the level of growth you want and the price you will accept match the investor's expectations? By what criterion do you judge the choice of not taking investment?
- CH07: Where is the deepest point of cumulative operating loss in the projected P&L, and what is the basis for the required funds and the sales quantity?
- CH06: How were price and cost set so that profit = (price − total cost) × sales quantity holds?
- CH05: Where do the interests of users collide, and who bears that cost?
- Appendices C and D: Do the sales outlets and channels have a basis for handing over the planned volume?
- CH08: By what validation will you fill the places where the basis is empty?

## Key Takeaways

- The investor perspective is not a knack for raising money; it is a frame for reading a plan with questions about growth and exit added.
- Investors read technology, product, market, and founding team as evidence of the possibility of an EXIT.
- IR materials are read again as a document for review after the presentation ends, so they must be understandable without it and their basis verifiable.
- Highlights and risks, the basis for sales and supply, exit scenarios, and investment terms are each written as a pair with their basis.
- Whether to take investment is a question that comes before writing the plan well: it is about settling the goal and the reason and the price in equity you will accept, and that criterion is set in CH01.
- This appendix is not legal, tax, or investment advice. Investment-trend statistics, valuation values, ownership percentages, and contract, tax-law, and fund rules are unconfirmed and are not included.
