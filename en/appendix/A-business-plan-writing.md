# Appendix A. Business Plan Writing in Practice

> This appendix is supplementary material drawn from the standard lecture materials that organize the author's teaching and consulting results, not from the handbook body (which is Knowledge-Module based).

## Questions This Chapter Answers

- What questions should you be able to answer before writing a single sentence?
- How do you design the table of contents and the scenario, and why does the writing order differ from the order of the table of contents?
- How do you write evidence instead of assertions, and mark the evidence status of every number?
- What should you check before submission?

## 1. Core Concepts

CH08 summarized the business plan document as a by-product built from the back data accumulated during validation. This appendix covers how to assemble that back data into a document. The strength of a business plan lies not in smooth prose but in the evidence that explains "why this decision was made." If you embellish a story without evidence, you end up with a business novel, so most of the work of writing is planning work done before writing.

A document has a reader, and the reader has a decision to make with it. When the purpose differs — startup support, R&D, sales, investment — the decision-maker and the core question differ. By comparison, a business plan relatively explains feasibility of execution, while investment materials relatively explain growth potential and capital efficiency. Only after you decide "who is reading this to make which decision" can you decide which evidence goes first.

Even when the form changes, the logic of the business does not change much. A business plan is not about filling in a form but about answering questions without a break, and what the reader looks at most is whether the answer to an earlier question leads into the answer to the next.

## 2. Key Frameworks and Mental Models

### Five Questions to Check Before Writing

What do we sell, and at what price? Who buys, and why? What is the market climate? How many can we sell? How much does it cost to make and sell? Research any question you cannot yet answer before writing sentences.

### Scenario Design and Writing Order

The work has two stages. The first is designing the table of contents. Use the one proposed by the submitting body as the baseline, and compose your own if there is none. For each item, write what you will say and the numbers it connects to. Estimated costs, estimated SG&A, and expected revenue come together to become the basis for estimated operating profit. The second stage is to confirm the supporting materials and fill in the core message for each item, gathering more material where it falls short. Build the execution plan and the projected financials in a spreadsheet so that the numbers in the sentences come out of calculations, and keep the files separated with version names.

The writing order differs from the order of the table of contents. In the local startup plan exercise, the sections were written in this order: customers and trade area, product and price, numbers, marketing, schedule, and founder introduction, with the summary written last. If you write the summary first, you end up forcing the rest to fit the summary.

### Write the Core in Sentences First

First write the core in a single paragraph of about seven sentences. Answer six questions, one each: the goal, the customer and the problem, the value and differentiation, the business structure, the economics, and the execution and evidence (editorial synthesis). The order is sentences, evidence, numbers, tables and diagrams, and the final document; a long plan is a document that expands the evidence and numbers behind these sentences.

### Evidence Instead of Assertions

"Excellent," "no competitors," and "high marketability" are assertions. Following the same principle as CH04's framework for turning assertions into evidence, replace them with conditions and figures the customer can judge, evidence you investigated directly, and the link between the problem, the support, and the results. A goal made only of abstract nouns is weak; it needs verbs, figures, and deliverables together. Build market size not as "1% of the market" but as price × quantity per CH07, and write the evidence for each number you multiply.

### Numbers and Evidence Status

Write the source and the date of research next to each number and mark its status. What you confirmed directly is a fact, what you estimated is an assumption, and what you have not confirmed is to be verified. Public data is an estimate, so cross-check it against values you counted directly before citing it. Revenue can be worked out backward from the value required by the fixed costs, rather than treated as a forecast. Fix the monthly fixed costs, check that the founder's own labor cost is included, and divide by the money left per unit to find the number of sales required. The calculation procedure follows CH06, and the values are left as ○.

### The Evaluator's Perspective: Concept Only

The evaluator asks different questions at each stage. In the written review, they look at evidence and consistency; in the in-person review, at explanation and defense; and at the end, at whether the goals written in the plan were achieved. So numbers and goals must say the same thing at every stage, and the plan's goals become the final criteria for judgment. In a review such as a small-grant program that gives seed money to a single experiment, the deliverable, what has already been done, and the later expansion must be visible together with numbers, dates, and supporting proof. Evaluation sheets differ by program and by year, so check them directly in the call for proposals.

## 3. Application Process

1. **Decide the reader and the decision.** Write in one line who is reading in order to make which decision.
2. **Answer the five questions before writing.** Turn whatever remains as to be verified into research tasks.
3. **Design the table-of-contents scenario.** Using the submitting body's table of contents as the baseline, write for each item a one-sentence core message, the connected numbers, and whether supporting material exists.
4. **Fill in the evidence.** Draw it from the back data, and secure whatever is missing through research or small experiments.
5. **Write the core paragraph first.** Answer the six questions, one each.
6. **Write the body in the writing order.** Write in the order of customer and problem, product and price, numbers, and execution, and rewrite the summary last.
7. **Check before submission.** Confirm the following: the source and research date of each number, and consistency among the body, the tables, and the calculation files; the totals of required funds and the funding plan; the distinction between completed, in progress, and planned; an execution plan written as hypotheses and metrics; risks with trigger conditions and responses; and a summary that reflects the body.

## 4. Common Judgment Errors

- **The error of filling in the form first**: Filling in the table of contents without the reader's decision question leaves the parts disconnected.
- **The error of writing the summary first**: You end up forcing the rest to fit the summary.
- **The error of ending with assertions**: "No competitors" is read as a signal that no research was done.
- **The error of writing a list of activities as the execution plan**: The hypotheses to be confirmed and the data to be measured are missing.
- **The error of writing "no risks"**: It reads as a plan that was never reviewed.
- **The error of leaving the founder's labor cost out of costs**: You mistake your own salary for profit.
- **The error of writing only in the future tense**: If what has already been done is not visible, trust falls (see the progress-status distinction in CH08).
- **The error of treating another program's evaluation criteria as a general rule**: The criteria differ with each call for proposals.

## 5. Practical Application Questions

- Who is the reader of this document, and which decision are they reading it to make?
- Which of the five questions before writing is still unanswered, and is the status of each answer a fact or an assumption?
- Where are the sentences written only as assertions, and what evidence could replace them?
- Does every number carry its source, research date, and evidence status?

## Key Takeaways

- The business plan is an assembly of the back data from the validation process, and most of the work of writing is planning done before writing.
- Because the reader and the decision question differ by document, decide the reader first, and answer the five questions before writing.
- Design the table of contents as a scenario; the writing order differs from the order of the table of contents. Write the summary last.
- Write the core in a single paragraph first, and replace assertions with figures and research evidence.
- Attach the source, research date, and evidence status to each number, and make the totals match across the body, the calculations, and the funding table.
- Because evaluation criteria differ by program, write the evaluator's perspective as a concept only, and confirm it in the call for proposals.
