# Appendix D. B2B and B2G Sales: The Basic Framework

> This appendix is supplementary material drawn from the standard lecture materials that organize the author's teaching and consulting results, not from the handbook body (which is Knowledge-Module based). The sales stages and proposal structure are rules of thumb.

## Questions This Chapter Answers

- When selling to B2B and B2G customers, how do you map out "who decides"?
- On what basis do you choose between direct proposal sales and indirect channels?
- Through which segments does a list price become the money actually received?
- Why does a small pilot adoption come before a large investment or a large commitment?
- Into what stages can sales be divided, and what must a proposal show?

## 1. Core Concepts

CH03 covered the separation of User, Buyer, and Payer in B2B and B2G and their purchasing logic, CH06 covered volume pricing and procurement price structure, and CH07 covered the basis for Qty by transaction type. This appendix carries that diagnosis over to the question on the sales floor: whom to approach, how, and in what order to move through the sales process.

A consumer sees and decides directly, but in a company or institution the person who uses, the purchasing officer, and the approver can be separate people. So B2B and B2G sales is not about publicizing a good product but about approaching the purchasing officer and the purchasing procedure. Companies and institutions do not buy just because something is "good." The question running through this whole chapter is not "whom do we sell to?" but "who decides?"

## 2. Key Frameworks and Mental Models

### The buyer map

The buyer map is a table with four boxes (the user, the purchasing officer, the approver, and the problem to solve), in which you write one line each for who they are, what they want, what we can give, and what stands in the way. How to read it is simple. The user looks at convenience, the purchasing officer at terms and risk, and the approver at effect and budget. Take care not to treat only the purchasing officer as the buyer. A line-department head, a representative, or a decision-making committee can play the buyer role, and only when you find the people who influence the decision and the others involved do each one's value factors become visible. Do not leave an unknown box empty; write "unknown" and note how you will find out.

Even for the same product, the "reason it is good" differs depending on who buys. In B2B, willingness to pay arises when the buyer resonates with one of the values: performance gain, cost reduction, or strategic advantage. B2G first checks "how it can be bought" rather than a good product. Without a budget it cannot be bought, and without eligibility one cannot bid or contract. Specific limits and procedures change from time to time, so verify them in official sources.

### Direct proposal sales and indirect channels

Channel choice is a balance between market access and profit margin per unit. Direct proposal sales cuts intermediate stages and lowers the fee burden, but you must handle everything from prospecting to contract yourself. An indirect channel widens your reach, but supply-price negotiation and fees enter the price structure. Neither is always better; judge by the target customer, product characteristics, available resources, and pace of growth.

### From list price to actual sale price

The two equations of CH06 matter even more in B2B and B2G. In public procurement or large transactions, the number on the price list differs from the number that lands in your account. Read the segments in the following order.

| Step | Description | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| List price | The price written in the proposal or price list | ○ |
| Discount | Reduction according to transaction terms | −○ |
| Commission and fees | The share taken out in the procurement, consignment, or distribution process | −○ |
| Actual sale price | The amount actually received | ○ |
| Variable costs | Costs that grow together with that deal | −○ |
| Contribution margin per deal | The share left when the sale is over | ○ |

If you do not know the gap between the list price and the price actually received, you can win a contract and still have nothing left. In B2B volume pricing, volume changes the unit price. Even when you lower the unit price, you must design the margin structure first, and the premise on which the lowered unit price holds is that the volume is confirmed. So before "at what price do we sell?", ask "how many are confirmed?" The calculation procedure is handed over to CH06 and *Price Is Strategy*.

### A pilot that starts small

Make large investments or large commitments after confirming the intent to buy. Before investing in certification or packaging, calculate with contribution margin how many months it takes to recover the investment, and first confirm the intent to buy and the terms through a small-quantity pilot delivery. For the pilot, write down in numbers in advance the quantity, the period, and the pass criteria, along with how to stop and which alternative channel to move to if the criteria are not met. This is the idea of a pilot that checks the single riskiest hypothesis on a small scale, and the framework for validation design is covered in CH08.

### The 7 sales steps and the proposal (rules of thumb)

Sales does not end in one go. It can be divided into seven steps: finding targets, qualification review, problem diagnosis, proposal, negotiation, contract, and after-sales management. This division is a general structure and differs by industry. It is a rule of thumb, not evidence-based data. If you set a criterion for "done" at each step, the stage where you have stalled shows up as the first problem to solve.

A proposal is a document that shows a "safe decision," not a "good product." The purchasing officer is responsible for the decision, so they look not only for effect but also for grounds that reduce risk. State in numbers the value we can give among performance, cost, and strategy, and propose a way to start small, such as a pilot adoption, alongside it. This checklist is also a rule of thumb.

## 3. Application Process

1. **Draw the buyer map.** Write the user, the purchasing officer, the approver, and the problem to solve, and fill unknown boxes with "unknown" and the way to check.
2. **Translate it into the buyer's language.** Write the effect in the language of cost, performance, and risk, and for B2G confirm how it can be bought.
3. **Choose the channel.** Compare direct and indirect on profit margin, access, and the work you can take on.
4. **Draw the price segments.** Follow from the list price to the actual sale price and the contribution margin per deal, and for B2B ask about the confirmed volume first.
5. **Design a small first proposal.** Set the pilot's quantity, period, and pass and stop criteria.
6. **Check the sales stages.** Write the "done" criterion for each step and find the stalled stage, then structure the proposal from the viewpoint of a safe decision.

## 4. Common Judgment Errors

- **Seeing only the purchasing officer as the buyer**: There may be a separate line-department head, representative, committee, or approver. If you meet only the user, you miss why praise from the field does not turn into a purchase.
- **Assuming direct sales is always more profitable**: The profit margin may rise, but you must take on sales yourself and your access narrows.
- **Treating the list price and the price actually received as the same**: Discounts, commissions, and fees all come out of your share.
- **Promising a bulk unit price before the volume is confirmed**: A lowered unit price presumes the volume.
- **Making a large investment before confirming the intent to buy**: If you do not calculate the payback period, it is hard to undo.
- **Seeing B2G as only a matter of persuading with the product**: Without a budget or eligibility, there is no way to buy even a good product.

## 5. Practical Application Questions

- Are the person who uses my product, the person who pays, and the person who approves the same? Of the three, who do we know least right now, and how can we find out?
- How many sales staff can our team take on directly?
- What are the actual sale price after subtracting discounts and fees from the list price, and the contribution margin per deal?
- If the order quantity is halved, what must this price become?
- For B2G, where might we be blocked: budget or eligibility?
- In the first proposal, how can the buyer start small, and how do we stop if the criteria are not met?
- At which of the seven steps is our sales stalled?

## Key Takeaways

- B2B and B2G sales starts not from "whom do we sell to?" but from "who decides?"
- Draw the buyer map with the user, the purchasing officer, the approver, and the problem to solve, and leave unknown boxes as "unknown" with a way to check.
- Direct proposal sales and indirect channels are a balance between market access and profit margin per unit, and neither is always superior.
- The list price and the price actually received differ. Draw the segments before selling, and in B2B ask for volume confirmation before the unit price.
- Before a large investment, calculate the payback period, and confirm the intent to buy and the terms with a small-quantity pilot.
- The 7 sales steps and the "safe decision" structure of the proposal are rules of thumb, to be adapted to the industry.
