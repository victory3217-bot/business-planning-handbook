# Appendix B. Customer Interviews, Observation, and Empathy Research

> This appendix is supplementary material drawn from the standard lecture materials that organize the author's teaching and consulting results, not from the handbook body (which is Knowledge-Module based).

## Questions This Chapter Answers

- How do you translate the questions and targets set in CH03 into an actual interview questionnaire?
- Why should what the customer said, what the customer did, and the recorder's interpretation be written separately?
- Why are interviews alone not enough, and what does observation complement?
- How do you group scattered records and carry them forward as a single problem statement?
- When and for what purpose do you use the empathy map, the user journey map, and the persona?

## 1. Core Concepts

Empathy is putting yourself in the customer's position and seeing through the customer's eyes and heart. The interview and observation records gathered in the empathy stage become the material for finding the problem in the later analysis stage.

A questionnaire is not a list of questions but a structure that descends backward from the task to be performed. The task to be performed, the key questions, and the content to be learned and the research methods are decided first, and the interview questions actually to be asked are written last. This translates CH03's "build backward from the decision to be changed" into the shape of a single questionnaire page.

Records are written in three layers. The customer's words are recorded as they were said; the customer's behavior and emotions are recorded separately; and the interpretations and insights the recorder came up with are marked distinctly. If words and interpretations are mixed, you cannot later separate where the customer's words end and where our guesses begin.

Interviews and observation complement each other. An interview is research done by asking and listening in a calm setting, so you may miss important information or misunderstand it. Observation shows natural behavior, reactions, and usage paths relatively accurately. When words and behavior differ, that gap is exactly where you should dig deeper. Observation looks for three things: Needs, Unmet Needs, and Pain Point. The Pain Point connects to CH03's existence and severity of the problem.

## 2. Key Frameworks and Mental Models

**Reverse-engineering the questionnaire.** Fill in the four rows below from top to bottom.

| Step | What to write | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Task to be performed | What this research is meant to accomplish | Is it one sentence? |
| Key questions | The questions that must be answered to perform the task | Do they connect to the decision to be changed? |
| Content to learn and methods | The content needed to answer the key questions, and for each, whether an interview can reveal it or observation is needed | Is a method attached? |
| Interview questions | The questions to actually ask | Did every one come from the rows above? |

Questions basically begin with when, why, and how, and take a form that has the person describe a recent experience in detail and in sequence; adapt them to the type of target. There is no fixed order for an interview, but it generally runs through introduction, building rapport, main questions, and closing.

**Record format.** Write one item per line and separate the columns. The column names are the editor's synthesis.

| No. | Customer's words (verbatim) | Behavior / emotion | Insight (marked separately) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | As heard | As seen | The recorder's interpretation |

**Observation notes.** Write the points to watch in the notes in advance and then begin. Keep the subject from becoming aware of being observed, and ask about anything you are curious about in a separate interview. Take many photos where possible. Consent for photographing and recording, and personal data protection, must be checked separately.

**Problem-definition tools, compared on one page.** The tools for organizing the collected records can be used alone, together, or in sequence.

| Tool | What it does | Input | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Affinity diagram method | Splits the records into single lines and groups similar ones together to find patterns | Records from multiple people | Named groups |
| Empathy map | Gathers one user's seeing, hearing, saying and doing, thinking and feeling, along with pains and expectations, onto one sheet | One person's records | Patterns, pains, expectations, potential deep needs |
| User journey map | Lays the experience out by stage (before, during, after) and writes expectations, actions, emotions, discomforts, and satisfactions to find the moments felt most strongly | The flow of the experience | Points to focus on |
| Persona | Portrays a fictional person representing a user type through demographics, behavior, personality, mindset, values, interests, needs, and goals | Results of the tools above | A single sheet that lets the team picture the same person |

Write the journey map from the participant's point of view, not the operator's. In a persona, do not invent fields that have no basis in the research.

## 3. Application Process

1. **Decide the task to be performed and the key questions.** Start from the decision you want to change among the hypotheses in CH03.
2. **Attach a method to each piece of content to be learned.** Separate what an interview can reveal from what only observation can confirm, and put both in one plan.
3. **Write the interview questions.** Delete any question that does not answer a key question.
4. **Conduct the interview and organize it right away.** Take down the record without interpretation, fill in gaps before memory fades, and mark interpretations separately.
5. **Observe.** Write the points to watch first, then look for Needs, Unmet Needs, and Pain Points. Mark where words and behavior differed.
6. **Split the records into single lines and group them.** Write one thought per note, group similar ones, and name each group. Name it not for a solution but for the shared reason behind what the customer said.
7. **Pick one group and write it as a single problem statement.** Use the frame "Who, in what situation, because of what, feels what discomfort (Needs)," interpret the hidden cause behind the visible phenomenon, and then turn it into the sentence.
8. **Connect it to severity and attributes.** Judge severity by frequency, current alternatives, and loss, and translate the language of the field into attribute language (CH03). Do not jump to a solution.

## 4. Common Judgment Errors

- Making the list of questions first, so that questions unrelated to the key questions get mixed in.
- Asking through interviews alone even about content that requires observation.
- Mixing the customer's words, behavior, and the recorder's interpretation on one line. Later, you cannot tell evidence from guesswork.
- Organizing the records only after time has passed following the interview, and relying on memory.
- Trusting only what was heard in the interview and not confirming it by observation.
- Naming a group for a solution. A group's name should be the shared reason behind what the customer said.
- Trying to fix the visible phenomenon right away without interpreting the hidden cause.
- Filling interpretive fields such as thoughts and feelings as if they were facts. Words are the original text, behavior is observation, and the rest is inference.

## 5. Practical Application Questions

- What decision will this research change, and have you written down a research method for each piece of content to be learned?
- In the records, are the customer's words, behavior and emotion, and your own interpretation kept separate?
- Where did the customer's words and behavior differ, and what does that gap mean?
- Is each group named for the shared reason behind what the customer said?
- Does your problem statement include the who, the situation, and the reason?

## Key Takeaways

- Build the questionnaire backward, in the order of task to be performed, key questions, content to learn and methods, and interview questions.
- Record the customer's words, behavior and emotion, and insight separately, and organize them right after the interview.
- Interviews miss things, so complement them with observation. The gap between words and behavior is a clue.
- In observation, write the points to watch in advance and look for Needs, Unmet Needs, and Pain Points.
- Group the records line by line, name the groups by shared reason, and then connect them to a single problem statement, severity, and attributes.
- The empathy map, user journey map, and persona are organizing tools; do not fill in fields that have no basis.
